DraftKings Daily Fantasy — Tournament Construction Methodology
Baseball build of the portable DFS process. Ledger current through Rule #53 (propagated from the July 28, 2026 post-mortem), then consolidated: 5 rules folded, 48 active. One further candidate rule declined under governance. Supersedes v3.

THE TWO THINGS THAT ARE THE FRAMEWORK — (1) Contest-shape first (Section 5). Classify the payout curve before any player decision. Same player pool + wrong shape = cash and still lose money. (2) Post-mortem cadence (Section 9). Most leaks are a nameable allocation error — a plug, not a rebuild. But a leak can also be a STANDING RULE pointing the wrong way (June 30); when two+ losses share a root, audit the rules themselves.
NEW IN v4 — the July 28 loss was caused by a rule, not an allocation. Rule #45 excluded three of the slate’s five best offenses one day after Rule #47 established that single factors are weights. v4 resolves that contradiction (#50) and converts the two clauses of #44 that were written but never enforced into hard build constraints (#49, #52).
0 — Ground-Truth the Scoring
Every edge must trace to a specific line in the DK scoring rules. Re-confirm roster construction each slate before building.

Roster: 2 P, 1 C, 1 1B, 1 2B, 1 3B, 1 SS, 3 OF. $50,000 cap; standing floor $48,500. Max 5 hitters from one team — the reason the 5-man stack is the organizing unit. Lineup spans ≥2 games. A pitcher may sit with his own team’s bat stack (positive correlation).
THE EDGE QUESTION — Strikeouts (+2): floor that builds regardless of the win. Walks + HBP (+2): cheap plate-appearance value the field ignores chasing power. The total-base ladder (3/5/8/10): doubles and triples are over-rewarded vs how the field prices contact.
1 — The Structural Edge: Casual-Field Behavior
The casual field over-owns famous names (50–70%) and underprices role and floor. Underweight fame in its obvious spots; overweight the underowned contributor.
— Fade the obvious ace + famous offense — but classify WHY the arm is chalk first (Rule #38). Narrative chalk fades hard; structural chalk gets eaten.
— Target leverage stacks of underowned offenses in good park/total spots, and the contrarian SP with real ceiling (Rule #8).
— CAVEAT ADDED v4: all of the above assumes a casual field. In small, high-buy-in fields the crowd is already dispersed and there is little fame premium to harvest — see Rule #51.
5 — Contest Shape (classify BEFORE any player decision)
Determine the payout curve first. Steep top-heavy (25%+ of pool to 1st) = ceiling-first, dead bats tolerated. Flat/wide = consistency.
Below ~1,000 entries: switch the optimizer objective per Rule #44, AND enforce the hard dead-bat cap (#49) and the ceiling-bat requirement (#52). These are build constraints, not weights.
FIELD SIZE SETS THE OWNERSHIP TRANSFORM BEFORE ANY LEVERAGE MATH: above ~500 entries apply Rule #42 (chalk amplifies); below ~500 apply Rule #51 (chalk deflates, tails inflate) and expect leverage to be worth very little.
7 — validate() — run on every file before handoff
— Positions sourced from the DK entries file, parsing COMBINED eligibility (‘1B/OF’) correctly (Rule #6).
— PLAYER ID KEYING (added v4): resolve every DK player ID on the three-part key (name, team, is-pitcher). Name-only keys silently collide on 20-team slates. 7/28 had two Jose Fermins; the name-keyed map wrote the PITCHER’s ID into a 3B and an OF slot. DK rejected it only because the collision was hitter-on-pitcher — a hitter-on-hitter collision uploads clean and rosters the wrong player.
— Salary floor $48,500, cap $50,000 (Rule #2). Roster legality: 10 slots; max 5 hitters/team; spans ≥2 games.
— Negative-correlation check: no hitter facing a rostered pitcher.
— DEAD-BAT CAP (Rule #49, added v4): below ~1,000 entries, REJECT any lineup with more than one hitter under the dk25 floor. Hard fail, not a warning.
— CEILING BAT (Rule #52, added v4): in small-field WTA, REJECT any lineup whose highest-dk95 hitter falls under the 25-point threshold.
— TOP-5 COVERAGE (Rule #50, added v4): REJECT any PORTFOLIO with zero exposure to a team ranked top 5 by projected stack median or implied total.
— BLOCKED-SIGNAL ADJUDICATION (Rule #53, added v4): refuse handoff while any rescan-surfaced sub-2% / xSLG ≥ .45 bat remains unadjudicated in the discarded-signal log.
— STACK-SIDE TEAM-IDENTITY ASSERTION (Rule #45, retained): for each stack, resolve the OPPOSING SP from the game string and assert his team ≠ the stack’s team. Fail the build. (The CLEAR/BLOCK gate verdict is retired — see #50.)
— WEATHER-STATE CHECK (Rule #48): any game in an ANNOUNCED delay at lock caps at 3 bats per lineup and ≤1/3 of portfolio stacks.
— Single-player hitter cap 50% (Rule #13); single-SP cap ~20% (Rule #33). Both unenforceable below ~6 lineups — the granularity floor at n=3 is 33%.
— Lock-state confirmed batting order via the actual lineup card, never the projections status column. Hash dedup (Rule #5).
8 — Ownership Smell Test
— Distinguish concentrated chalk from distributed stack ownership. One or two bats at 25%+ is a fade signal; a popular TEAM whose bats are each 4–11% owned is NOT (Rule #29). The inverse also holds: an aggregate 130% with ZERO sub-10% bats is a true trap (BOS 7/27, 0.48× lift; BOS again 7/28, 13th of 18).
— Apply the ownership transform the moment the entry fee AND field size are known: #42 above ~500 entries, #51 below. Recompute the sub-4% dart pool AFTER the transform.
— VERIFY THE TRANSFORM AGAINST REALISED OWNERSHIP EVERY SLATE (added v4). Two slates of projected-vs-actual data is not a calibration curve. Log the ratios; #51’s multipliers are provisional.
— Reconcile projected ownership against betting odds. A strong play at near-zero ownership is usually a data error — floor it at 10–15%.
— Run the hero-chalk scan (Rule #37) and the IL-return scan (Rule #40) every slate.
THE CHALK-LIVE DETECTOR — Score the slate on four signals: (a) elite aces at 35%+ own in plus matchups; (b) compressed slate of small favorites; (c) the chalk stack’s individual bats are themselves low-owned; (d) the highest-ceiling stacks ARE the chalk. When ≥2 fire, mandate a chalk-anchored block of 25–30% (Rule #34). Then classify the chalk SP itself (Rule #38). Prototype: chalk_live_detector.py.
— DETECTOR SPLIT (added v4): score the chalk ARM and the chalk STACK separately. 7/28 fired 3 of 4 signals; the chalk ace (Williams) led all SPs at 33.35 while the chalk stack (CLE) finished LAST of 18. Signal (c) not firing is the tell that the stack half is a trap while the arm half is real.
9 — The Post-Mortem Engine (the flywheel)
After every slate: scoreboard vs peers; what you got right; what you got wrong ranked by cost; new rules adopted (evergreen, propagated); process notes.
— DISCARDED-SIGNAL LOG (added 7/27, enforces #46; HARDENED by #53 on 7/28): during the confirmed-lineup phase, every pre-slate thesis that gets dropped receives one line stating which mechanism died and which survived. WRITE IT DURING THE RESCAN, NOT AFTER. Four consecutive slates have now been damaged by a correctly-identified signal dying silently in this phase.
— CROSS-ENTRY IS A FLOOR MEASUREMENT, NEVER VALIDATION (added v4). When one lineup set is entered into two contest shapes, the results are ONE observation, not two. A respectable cash rate in the wider field does not partially redeem a set that produced nothing in the shape it was built for — min-cashes measure floor, and floor is not the objective in either shape. Read the cross-entry only as a percentile reading on the portfolio. Cf. 6/16 and 7/28.
THE DISCIPLINE — amended June 30 — Most leaks are a single, nameable allocation error. BUT a leak can be a STANDING RULE pointing the wrong way (6/30: Rule #1 deleted the winning arm). When two consecutive+ losses share a root, audit the RULES, not the allocations. Over-fitting to a past sample is itself a leak.
— COROLLARY ADDED v4: when a post-mortem’s leading leak is a rule adopted within the previous two slates, the consolidation pass is MANDATORY before the next build, not deferred to the next review.

11 — Ledger Governance
The portable operating model warns that a rulebook with more rules than observed slates is overfit by construction, and that a post-mortem process which only ADDS rules is a one-way ratchet. v3 flagged this at 48 rules against ~15 slates as "a live risk, not a strength." On July 28 that risk materialised and cost a $1,900 entry: Rule #45 (adopted 7/27) made a single matchup factor a hard exclusion one day after Rule #47 (adopted 7/27) established that single factors are weights. The contradiction excluded three of the slate’s five best offenses.
CONSOLIDATION PASS EXECUTED IN v4 — the first net reduction in ledger size:
— #1, #31, #47 and the gate clause of #45 → FOLDED INTO #50 (single-factor screens are weights, never gates). Evidence lines preserved.
— #15 → FOLDED INTO #3 (never zero a team; #15 was the same rule with an operational detail).
— #32 → FOLDED INTO #29 (aggregate vs individual ownership; v3 already flagged these as the same rule stated twice).
— Net: 53 numbered, 5 folded, 48 ACTIVE. Ratio improved from 48/15 to 48/16 and, more importantly, the internal contradiction is gone.

Standing governance rules:
— Every rule carries a hit/miss record. Track which rules fire, and whether firing helped.
— A rule that has misfired and been amended twice is RETIRED, not amended a third time.
— Prefer fewer, stronger coverage rules to a growing thicket of allocation rules.
— Remaining consolidation candidates at the next review: #12/#37/#38 all govern chalk-SP treatment and could collapse into one classification rule with three branches; #29/#33 both cap concentration in different domains.
— ADDED v4: no new rule may be adopted in the same post-mortem cycle as a rule it contradicts. Before adopting, check the new rule against every rule added in the previous three post-mortems.
— FIRST APPLICATION, 7/28: a candidate rule on cross-entering one portfolio into two contest shapes was DECLINED. The finding was real but it was confirming evidence for #44 and Section 5, which already said it. The evidence was appended to #44 and one clause added to Section 9 instead. Logging declined rules is as important as logging adopted ones — it is the only visible proof the ratchet is off.
